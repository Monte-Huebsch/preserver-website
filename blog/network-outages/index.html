The Network Goes Down More Than You'd Think

You don't need to be off-grid for "no signal" to become a problem. In 2026, major cloud and telecom outages have become a weekly occurrence — and most field apps have no answer for it.

It's not just you

It's easy to assume a dropped connection is a local problem — bad reception, an overloaded tower, a building with thick walls. Often it isn't. Internet monitoring firm ThousandEyes tracked global outage activity through 2026 and found public cloud network outages spiking as much as 72–98% week-on-week at points during the year. Named incidents hit major providers directly: AT&T had three separate outages in September alone; Salesforce had a 4.5-hour outage the same month; Microsoft, Cogent, Cox and Charter all had their own disruptions across the year. These aren't obscure providers — they're the infrastructure most cloud-dependent apps quietly rely on.

What breaks when the cloud does

Most field-capture and note-taking apps assume the network is there. They sync as you go, save to the cloud by default, or need a live connection to even open. When the network — or the app's own backend — goes down, so does your ability to document anything, right when you might need to most: mid-inspection, mid-job, mid-incident.

Offline isn't just for remote work

The obvious cases are the ones already covered elsewhere — underground, in aircraft, on rural properties with no towers nearby. But the outages above didn't happen in remote areas. They happened to ordinary cloud services millions of people use every day, in cities, during business hours. Offline-first isn't just a feature for people working off-grid. It's insurance against infrastructure you don't control failing at a bad moment.
